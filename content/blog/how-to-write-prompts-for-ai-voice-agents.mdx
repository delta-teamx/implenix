Prompt engineering for voice AI is the difference between a deployment
that produces 30% missed-call recovery and one that produces 80%. The
prompt is the agent's operating system.

This is the operator's playbook from 200+ Implenix deployments. Same
7-section structure regardless of industry. The content varies; the
structure does not.

## Who this is for

Operations leads, founders, and product managers writing or reviewing
the prompts that will power their AI voice agent. If you have already
deployed and your agent feels generic, the seven-section framework
below is the diagnostic checklist.

## The 7-section structure

Every production AI voice prompt has these sections in this order:

1. **System role** — who the agent is
2. **Tone and voice** — how the agent speaks
3. **Intake script** — what the agent captures
4. **Transfer rules** — when the agent escalates
5. **Escalation flows** — how the agent escalates
6. **Edge case responses** — how the agent handles surprises
7. **Do-not-do list** — what the agent must never do

## Section 1: System role

The identity layer. Single paragraph, written in second-person ("You
are..."). Covers:

- The business name and what it does
- The agent's name and role
- The primary objective of every call
- The boundary of the agent's responsibility

**Example for HVAC:**

> You are Avery, the virtual front-desk dispatcher for Northside HVAC,
> a five-truck residential and light-commercial HVAC operator serving
> the metro area. Your primary objective on every call is to qualify
> the caller's situation, capture full intake data, and either dispatch
> emergency service immediately or book a scheduled service
> appointment. You handle inbound from customers and prospective
> customers. You do NOT handle billing disputes, technical
> troubleshooting beyond basic, or commercial sales conversations —
> those route to the appropriate human team member.

The boundary line ("you do NOT handle") is as important as the
identity line.

## Section 2: Tone and voice

Industry-specific. The HVAC dispatcher sounds different from the
dental front desk who sounds different from the boutique law firm
intake person.

Specify:

- **Pace:** "Speak at a measured, professional pace. Do not rush."
- **Formality:** "Warm but professional. Address callers as 'Mr.' or
  'Ms.' unless they introduce themselves with a first name."
- **Empathy markers:** "When a caller describes an emergency,
  acknowledge it briefly before moving to dispatch logistics. 'I'm
  sorry that's happening — let me get a tech to you right away.'"
- **Language:** "Use plain English. Avoid technical HVAC jargon
  unless the caller introduces it first."
- **Pauses:** "Pause briefly after asking a question to give the
  caller time to think."

<Pull>Most prompts skip the tone section. The agent ends up sounding generic. The voice section is where 'feels like a real receptionist' is built.</Pull>

## Section 3: Intake script

The data the agent captures, in the order it should be asked, with
the exact phrasing.

For a HVAC service call, the script might be:

1. "Are you experiencing a heating or cooling issue right now? If
   yes — emergency triage path."
2. "Can I get the address where service is needed?"
3. "And the best callback number?"
4. "What's happening with the system? Is it not running at all, or
   running but not heating/cooling properly?"
5. "About how old is the unit?"
6. "Is anyone home now, or will someone be available when our tech
   arrives?"
7. "Have we serviced you before? Or is this your first time?"

Each data point feeds the CRM. The script is the agent's structured
data capture layer.

## Section 4: Transfer rules

Explicit conditions that trigger live transfer to a human. Written as
"if X, then transfer to Y."

Common transfer rules across industries:

1. **VIP allow-list:** "If the caller's phone number matches the VIP
   list, transfer immediately to [Name]."
2. **Urgent keywords:** "If the caller says any of: 'emergency,'
   'flooding,' 'no heat with kids,' 'no AC with elderly,' 'smell
   gas,' 'carbon monoxide' — flag as critical and transfer to on-call
   tech within 6 seconds."
3. **Sentiment triggers:** "If sentiment analysis detects extended
   distress, anger, or crying, transfer to a human within 30
   seconds."
4. **Off-script questions:** "If the caller asks about pricing for
   commercial installations over $5,000, transfer to the sales
   manager."
5. **Explicit request:** "If the caller says 'I want to talk to a
   person' or any variant, transfer immediately. Do not push back."

Document each rule precisely. The agent's transfer behavior is only
as good as your rules.

## Section 5: Escalation flows

For each transfer rule, specify what happens.

**Example escalation flow for emergency keyword:**

1. Agent says: "I'm sorry that's happening. I'm connecting you to our
   on-call tech right now."
2. Agent triggers SIP transfer to on-call rotation phone
3. If first ring fails, agent rolls to backup tech in 8 seconds
4. If backup fails, agent rolls to owner in 8 more seconds
5. If all fail, agent says: "I'm having trouble connecting you live —
   I've sent an urgent text to our team and they'll call you back
   within 5 minutes. In the meantime, if this is a gas leak or
   carbon monoxide concern, please leave the house and call 911."

The fallback flow is where prompts fail in production. Always specify
what happens when the human team does not pick up.

## Section 6: Edge case responses

The 30-60 edge cases that real callers will throw at the agent. A
short response template for each.

Categories of edge cases:

- **Pricing questions:** "I can't give exact pricing on the call —
  our tech provides quotes on-site. Want me to book a free
  assessment?"
- **Confused callers:** "Let me help you figure out what you need.
  Are you calling because something in your home stopped working,
  because you want to make an appointment, or for something else?"
- **Angry callers:** "I hear you, that's frustrating. Let me get you
  to someone who can resolve this directly — give me one moment."
- **Off-topic questions:** "I'm here to help with [Business]'s
  service — I can't help with [topic]. Is there anything related to
  [Business] I can help with?"
- **Repeat callers asking same thing:** "I see we spoke earlier
  today. Let me pull up the notes — one moment."
- **Callers gaming the system:** "I'm not able to discuss that — let
  me connect you to someone who can."

Treat the edge case list as a living document. After every deployment
month, review recordings and add new edge cases to the prompt.

## Section 7: Do-not-do list

Hard rules. Written as numbered prohibitions.

Examples:

1. Do NOT provide medical, legal, or financial advice under any
   circumstance.
2. Do NOT commit to specific pricing on the call.
3. Do NOT discuss other customers or their accounts.
4. Do NOT speculate about issues outside the agent's scope.
5. Do NOT use political, religious, or controversial commentary.
6. Do NOT promise that a specific human will call back within a
   specific time unless that human has confirmed availability.
7. Do NOT make up information when asked something the agent does not
   know — say "I don't have that information, but I can connect you
   to someone who does."

The do-not list is the safety layer. Tighter rules produce more
predictable agents.

## How to maintain consistency in AI agent voice and style

Three practices that keep the agent on-brand over time:

1. **Version-control the prompt.** Every change goes through git.
   The prompt is code.
2. **Review 5-10 random recordings per week.** Note where the agent
   drifted. Update the prompt or the edge case list.
3. **Run weekly diff against production behavior.** When a vendor
   updates the underlying model, regression-test the agent against
   100 saved test calls.

The agent's voice consistency is an operational practice, not a
one-time setup.

## How AI agents learn processes without code

The natural follow-on question. Modern AI voice agents do not require
custom code for each business process — they learn from:

1. **The prompt** (the structured 7-section document above)
2. **Tool definitions** (what APIs the agent can call and what each
   one does)
3. **RAG knowledge base** (your business documents, FAQs, pricing
   sheets — the agent retrieves these at runtime)
4. **Few-shot examples** (sample successful calls included in the
   prompt to demonstrate the desired behavior)

Together these four inputs replace what would have been thousands of
lines of custom code in earlier-generation systems.

## What to do next

If your current AI voice agent feels generic, the diagnostic is to
audit it against the seven sections above. The gaps are usually in
sections 2 (tone), 5 (escalation flows), and 6 (edge cases).

If you're starting from scratch, the 7-section framework is the right
structure to build to.
