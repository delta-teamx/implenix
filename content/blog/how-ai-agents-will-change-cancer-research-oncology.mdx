<Answer q="How are AI agents changing cancer research and oncology?">
Agentic AI is contributing to cancer research and oncology in five specific ways in 2026: literature synthesis across the millions of oncology papers published annually, clinical trial matching for patients (matching patient profiles to eligible trials at scale), imaging analysis assistance (radiology and pathology support that flags suspicious findings for physician review), treatment planning decision support (surfacing relevant guidelines and evidence), and administrative burden reduction in oncology practice (documentation, coordination). It does not replace oncologists, does not make diagnostic or treatment decisions autonomously, and is not "curing cancer" in the way headlines suggest.
</Answer>

Oncology is one of the medical specialties where agentic AI is
producing the clearest early results, and also one where the
hype outruns the reality most aggressively. I do not deploy
into cancer research or oncology care directly, that is a
specialized field with its own vendors and its own regulatory
framework, but I follow the space closely because operators in
adjacent medical fields want to understand what is happening.
This is the honest read on where agentic AI is actually moving
the needle in oncology.

## The five areas where AI is producing real value

### 1. Literature synthesis

The oncology literature is impossibly large. Roughly 200,000
papers are published in cancer research annually. No human
researcher or clinician can keep up. Agentic AI systems can
synthesize across this literature at scale, surfacing relevant
work for specific research questions or clinical cases.

Real production examples: OpenEvidence, Consensus, and various
research-focused tools. Also proprietary systems at major cancer
centers.

The value: researchers and clinicians can ask a question and
get a synthesis of relevant recent literature in minutes rather
than days. This does not replace deep reading, but it filters
what is worth reading.

### 2. Clinical trial matching

Matching patients to eligible clinical trials is one of the most
tractable and impactful AI applications in oncology. The
problem: there are thousands of active oncology trials, each
with dozens of inclusion and exclusion criteria. Matching a
specific patient's clinical profile to eligible trials manually
is time-intensive and error-prone.

Agentic AI systems read patient charts, extract relevant
clinical features (cancer type, stage, biomarkers, prior
treatments), and match against active trial databases. Some
systems can flag trials the oncologist may not have known
about.

Impact: increases trial enrollment (typically 5-10% in
programs with AI matching, versus 3-5% without), reduces the
"no trial available" false negatives, and connects patients
to potentially better options.

### 3. Imaging analysis assistance

AI in oncology imaging (radiology, pathology, dermatology) is
one of the most mature medical AI application areas. Multiple
FDA-cleared tools exist for:

- Detecting suspicious findings on mammograms, chest CTs,
  colonoscopies.
- Quantifying tumor burden on serial imaging.
- Grading pathology specimens for prognostic markers.
- Detecting melanoma from dermatologic photos.

These are decision-support tools. The radiologist, pathologist,
or dermatologist reviews the AI's findings and makes the final
call. AI catches things humans miss (fatigue, workload, subtle
findings) and humans catch things AI gets wrong.

### 4. Treatment planning decision support

Molecular tumor boards, where oncologists review the genomic
profiles of complex cases, increasingly use agentic AI to
surface relevant literature, similar cases, and treatment
guidelines. The AI helps the physician team consider options
they might have missed given the complexity of modern
oncology.

This is decision support, not decision replacement. The tumor
board's oncologists still decide what to recommend.

### 5. Administrative burden reduction

Oncology practice has particularly heavy administrative burden
documentation, prior authorizations, care coordination,
survivorship follow-up. Voice AI and agentic AI systems are
being deployed for:

- Ambient documentation during oncology visits (Nuance DAX,
  Abridge, similar).
- Prior authorization intake and submission workflows.
- Post-visit patient outreach for symptom monitoring.
- Coordination between oncologist, surgeon, radiation
  oncologist, and primary care.

These operational improvements do not "cure cancer" but they
reduce physician burnout and free clinical attention for the
work that requires it.

## What agentic AI does NOT do in oncology

- **Diagnose cancer autonomously.** All FDA-cleared imaging
  AI is decision-support with a physician in the loop.
- **Make treatment decisions.** Oncologists decide treatment,
  informed by AI-surfaced evidence.
- **Deliver bad news to patients.** Human conversation.
- **Handle end-of-life conversations.** Human, entirely.
- **Replace oncology nurses, patient navigators, or care
  coordinators.** These roles are being augmented, not
  replaced.
- **"Cure" cancer.** AI is a tool that helps humans do the
  research and care faster and better. It is not the cure.

## Real drug discovery contributions

Beyond clinical practice, agentic AI is contributing to cancer
drug discovery.

- **Target identification**, analyzing genomic and proteomic
  data to identify potential therapeutic targets.
- **Molecule design**, generative models suggesting candidate
  molecules for specific targets (some FDA IND clearances have
  come from AI-designed molecules).
- **Trial design**, using historical trial data to design
  more efficient studies.
- **Toxicity prediction**, flagging compounds likely to have
  problematic side effect profiles before they enter clinical
  testing.

These contributions are compressing timelines and reducing
costs in ways that will eventually flow through to patient
outcomes, but the timelines are still measured in years.

## The regulatory picture

FDA regulates most oncology AI as medical devices (Software as
a Medical Device, SaMD). Requirements include:
- Validation studies demonstrating safety and efficacy.
- Post-market surveillance.
- Clear labeling of intended use.
- Physician-in-the-loop for diagnostic decisions.

The FDA has cleared over 100 AI-based devices for oncology
applications by 2026. The regulatory framework is mature and
active.

## The equity question

AI in oncology can either widen or narrow disparities in care.

- **Narrow disparities** by making expert-level decision
  support available at community cancer centers that cannot
  staff specialty oncologists.
- **Widen disparities** if AI is only available at
  well-resourced academic medical centers.
- **Widen disparities** if training data does not represent
  diverse patient populations, leading to worse performance
  on underrepresented groups.

Responsible deployment addresses these actively. Reputable
oncology AI vendors publish equity metrics and work on
population-representative training data.

## What the near-term outlook looks like

Realistic 3-5 year trajectory:
- **Broader deployment** of proven decision support at more
  cancer centers.
- **Better trial matching** with automated re-matching as
  patient status changes.
- **Improved imaging** with more subtypes of cancer covered
  and better pathology AI.
- **More administrative automation** freeing clinical time.
- **Better patient-facing tools** for symptom monitoring and
  care coordination.

Not on the trajectory:
- Autonomous diagnostic AI making decisions without physician
  review.
- "AI oncologist" replacing human oncologists.
- Overnight cures.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can AI predict who will get cancer?**
For specific cancers (breast, lung, some others), AI-based
risk models exist and are used clinically. They inform
screening intensity, not diagnostic decisions.

**Can AI read my scan and tell me if I have cancer?**
No, not directly. AI-assisted imaging is used by radiologists
to help them read scans. The radiologist gives you (or your
doctor) the interpretation.

**Are oncologists being replaced by AI?**
No. Oncology is one of the specialties with the greatest
projected shortage over the next decade. AI is being deployed
to help oncologists handle more patients better, not to
replace them.

**What is the difference between AI in oncology and general
medical AI?**
Oncology has particularly heavy documentation, complex
treatment decisions, extensive literature, and high stakes, making it a target-rich area for AI applications. The
regulatory framework is the same as general medical AI.

**How is patient data used?**
For clinical AI, data use is heavily regulated (HIPAA plus
research-specific frameworks). Reputable oncology AI vendors
have clear data governance policies.

## Where to go from here

For anyone in the oncology space evaluating AI, the practical
first step is not building custom, it is evaluating the
mature FDA-cleared decision support tools relevant to your
specialty. For patients, the practical value of oncology AI
today usually reaches them through their oncologist's tools
rather than as a direct patient product.
